It’s been a year and 1 month to date (as of me writing this) as a Lead Engineer. (Aka squad lead/team lead/senior engineer - whatever you prefer to call me). It’s also been a little while since I last wrote about anything useful. The reason for that is I’ve been getting my ass kicked by the role.

Now technical leadership is not as traditionally consistent across industry from my experience chatting to colleagues across the aisles. The role tends to be really catered towards what is needed - and to be honest - the purity of the utilitarian nature of this is one I can’t help but admire (except in those moments where the situational ambiguity is an absolute pain in my ass).

Today I write about 3 of the biggest lessons I’ve learnt or gotten much better at - and basically rules of thumb that have served me well - and hopefully will continue to.

# 1. People come first 

An obvious statement - but fucking hard to do right. 

If this is not an obvious statement and it’s not obvious because of your company culture and it not being “a part of your job description” - then let me make a rational arguments case for this.

a. Your remit (primarily) is the technical success of the platform and or applications within your scope

b. Technical delivery and the SDLC are the primary methodology to get this right (albeit not the only one - but I think we’re sufficiently passed arguing about this as an overall software engineering faculty)

c. Who are the indispensable drivers of this process? People.*

I hope this argument is enough to convince you my fellow maestro of logical reasoning and problem solving.

If the reason is not the above but rather “i don’t care about the people” - I hope this argument above helped you to care - but I would put money on you have some tough years ahead if not - and I’m not a betting man.

(If the reason is company culture - move. You deserve better.)

Putting people first is not about buying them pizza on late nights (note - I’m not saying anything about pay and reward - that’s different - that’s about the company treating its people right and is largely out of your control). It’s not about letting them work on what they find cool, it’s not even about being friends with them.

So far I’ve come to realise that it’s more about:

a. Understanding their chosen growth journey (which requires them to have one - which I’m not wholly confident is something I personally can help people with effectively yet - I’m way too self assured in what I do to help people that are confused about where they are and want to be. Here I seek help - see 3 below.)

b. Identifying and executing on opportunities to put them in places that are, in-turn opportunities for them to progress on their journey

c. Being their second - having their back. But never, NEVER firing their bullets for them. (They won’t learn to shoot straight unless they actually practice shooting)

d. Feedback to them their progress - whether good or bad (that’s not to say throw the book at them - not all bad/critical feedback can be delivered equally - this is the hardest part for me - being someone that prefers their feedback neat, giving feedback with some ice and exotic cordials is a skill I’m still learning)

This is the biggest lesson that I’m still learning. I’m grateful to have my own leaders be really admirably good at this and I learn from them everyday.

# 2. A delivery program ends - the user experience and developer experience stays 

I’m not saying be a purist about delivery. It’s admirable - but not often useful.

Technical debt is a business line of credit that is not subject to actuarial models. I’m sure actuaries could build a predictive model that could help with utility of when to incur debt and when not to - but for some reason they haven’t- which in itself is interesting (I think we still need to work on getting a few seats at a few tables).

What this means - is that risk of debt being a function of certainty of default (or lack there of) makes this formulaically a high risk liability. The catch is that engineers are usually the only people that truly know this and they usually only know this intuitively - which doesn’t make for a good PowerPoint to our more business functional colleagues. 

My solution so far - besides taking care and treading lightly of course, is to communicate! Don’t complain about it post fact - communicate your discomfort upfront - communicate “why” ideally with objectivity which I just explained is hard to do. 

The tip that has served me well personally to this end is understanding the reasons why things need to be done at some seemingly arbitrary deadline. Understanding breaths empathy and empathy is the most important tool to breaking down hostile relationships. 

In my experience, the result of you putting in the work can be 3 cases - best case (as in easier for you personally) is that - shit - the reason is actually a good one. There’s some time to market sensitivity for example. 

Middle case - you agree with the reason but still think that compromise will not lead to good outcomes - I’ll get to this in a minute. 

Worst case - the reason is actually objectively arbitrary - this is the hardest one - because you’ll need to argue, not because you’re a person of high character and you worship at the alter of engineering excellence- but simply because you’re being paid to do this, it’s your job friend. 

Your remit is the technical success of the platform/application - which brings us to the middle case. My advice - and really this is more inherited advice that I’ve been blessed with by good leaders in turn - is to be hyper objective and unemotional, but also extremely clear that this is not something you’re against or are pushing back on because of anything personal but rather your professional opinion and remit obligates you to do so because of the facts that you very clearly and unemotionally need to lay before the stakeholders/delivery managers.

Either they take it with grace, intelligence and with trust. Or they don’t. The outcome of doing this is that you will at least have a clear conscience (you’ll be fucked off pissed but leadership sometimes is looking at silver linings as you get pushed into the storm that has them).

# 3. Seek help - your job doesn’t operate in isolation

Unless you work for some government agency that probably tracks our phones and mines our data and your job contract consisted of more ndas then I’ve probably signed in my entire lifetime - you are allowed to ask for help.

Not confident handling a non-technical leadership conundrum? Seek help from someone. The key though - is seeking help from someone you’ve seen do this in a way that you admire.

I phrased that very specifically - not someone that objectively you’ve been told is good at this - but someone you’ve seen in action and literally admired.

Statistically- your admiration for how they’ve handled something is probably an indication that the methods they’ve used is something you’ve related with and you’ll thusly be more able to pallet working with that person more effectively.

Warning: Pay attention to why and what you admire about things. It often points to things you either find interesting or desperately need (or both)
